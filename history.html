A short history of photography

The camera is barely two centuries old, but the urge behind it — to hold onto a moment — is much older. Here's the short version of how we got from light through a pinhole to the photos in this portfolio.

1820s

The first fixed image

Joseph Nicéphore Niépce produced the earliest surviving photograph by exposing a treated pewter plate for several hours, capturing the view from his window. The process was slow and the results faint, but it proved an image from life could be permanently recorded.

1839

The daguerreotype

Louis Daguerre refined the process into something practical: a sharp, detailed image on a silvered copper plate, ready in minutes rather than hours. It launched photography as a public phenomenon almost overnight, and studios opened across Europe and America within months.

1888

Photography for everyone

George Eastman's Kodak camera came preloaded with flexible roll film and a simple promise: "You press the button, we do the rest." Photography moved out of professional studios and into ordinary hands for the first time.

1920s–1950s

The modern documentary eye

Compact 35mm cameras like the Leica made photography fast and unobtrusive. Photographers such as Henri Cartier-Bresson began chasing the "decisive moment" — the instant when a fleeting scene resolves into a picture worth keeping.

1975

The first digital sensor

An engineer at Kodak, Steven Sasson, built a prototype camera that recorded images not on film but as digital data — a bulky toaster-sized device that took 23 seconds to save one black-and-white photo. It seeded the entire digital shift to come.

2000s

The camera in every pocket

Digital sensors improved fast enough to fit inside phones, and photography became a constant, casual habit rather than an occasional event. Billions of photos are now taken daily — most of them never printed, all of them instant.

Today

Where this portfolio sits

Despite all the technology built since Niépce's window, the basic act hasn't changed: pointing a camera at something worth looking at twice. That's still the whole idea behind the photos on this site.
